Research Directions.

COEVOLUTIONARY EXTREMES.

Cuckoos provide an excellent system for studying coevolutionary interactions, as they are locked in an arms race with their hosts, evolving egg mimicry and plumage color traits to evade detection. My research has examined the genetic basis of cuckoo adaptations: revealing that maternal genes control eggshell color, while genes from both parents contribute to egg speckling. Similarly, female-limited traits—like gray vs. rufous plumage are also linked to the female genome and appear to be maintained by balancing selection over millions of years. In fact, plumage polymorphism has been maintained for so long that speciation has occured, with two species of cuckoo harboring the same genetic basis of this trait.

EVOLUTIONARY EPIGENETICS.

I study how epigenetic variation contributes to evolutionary divergence, which requires disentangling environmental and genetic contributions. In crows, methylation differences mostly mirrored genetic structure, implying limited independent evolution of epigenetic marks, although females (ZW sex chromosomes) compensate their lack of a Z chromosome (compared to males who are ZZ) by increasing chromatin accessibility rather than altering methylation. In contrast, Canada lynx show low genetic diversity but elevated methylation differentiation across climatic gradients, suggesting that epigenetic patterns can either track genomic divergence or respond to environmental selection, depending on the system.

IMPROVING WHOLE-GENOME COMPUTATIONAL INFERENCE.

I am currently investigating the domestication history of breadfruit and guava—two tropical crop species with complex polyploid genomes—using long-read sequencing technologies and genome assembly. Polyploid crops harbor multiple genome copies, sometimes even from different species. To facilitate scalable genome assembly across many cultivars, I have developed puzzler, a bioinformatic pipeline for reproducible and rapid chromosome-scale assembly.

MULTIMODAL GENOMICS.

I am also exploring how the integration of whole-genome data with GPS tracking, environmental layers, and paleoclimate reconstructions can illuminate long-term evolutionary stability and connect processes across long time frames. In cuckoos, combining genomic and movement datasets shows that climate-driven shifts in migration over roughly 800,000 years altered flyways and gene flow, promoting lineage divergence. This integrative framework links movement ecology with population genomics and provides a foundation for understanding how climatic and ecological transitions shape speciation over macroevolutionary timescales.
